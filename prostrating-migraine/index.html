What "prostrating" means in the VA migraine rating

Three of the four migraine rating levels use the word "prostrating". The regulation never says what it means. This page shows where the word appears, how VA's own manual describes it, and what that suggests you write down on a bad day.

An independent explainer, not from the VA and not legal advice. I'm Fiona, the maker of this log; I am not a veteran, a lawyer or an accredited representative. For help with a claim, accredited Veterans Service Organization representatives help for free. Find one on VA.gov.

Where the word appears

VA rates migraine under 38 CFR § 4.124a, Diagnostic Code 8100:

50%: With very frequent completely prostrating and prolonged attacks productive of severe economic inadaptability
30%: With characteristic prostrating attacks occurring on an average once a month over last several months
10%: With characteristic prostrating attacks averaging one in 2 months over last several months
0%: With less frequent attacks

The frequency the schedule counts is prostrating attacks. A month with ten milder headaches and one attack that stopped you functioning counts, for frequency, as one, though VA must still consider all your migraine symptoms.

The regulation doesn't define it

Nothing in § 4.124a explains the word. Board of Veterans' Appeals decisions say so in plain terms, and many of them fall back on a dictionary: "extreme exhaustion or powerlessness".

VA's adjudication manual, the M21-1, does give its raters a working description (Part V, Subpart iii, Chapter 12, Section A, Topic 3, change date September 14, 2023):

  • Prostrating: "causing extreme exhaustion, powerlessness, debilitation or incapacitation with substantial inability to engage in ordinary activities."
  • Completely prostrating (the 50% level): "extreme exhaustion or powerlessness with essentially total inability to engage in ordinary activities."

The manual changes from time to time. It guides VA's raters and does not bind the Board of Veterans' Appeals; it isn't the law. Read the current wording at the link above, or ask your representative, before you lean on it.

In plain words

Both descriptions come back to the same question. Could you carry on with your day, or did the attack stop you?

An attack you worked through with medication and a dim screen is a migraine. An attack that sent you to a dark room, unable to drive, cook or look after the kids, is the kind the word is reaching for. This is my reading, not a VA test, and no minimum duration is set anywhere. That is why this site's log never asks "was it prostrating?" It asks whether you had to stop what you were doing and lie down, which you can answer truthfully on the day without interpreting a regulation.

What to write down on an attack day

  • The date.
  • Whether you had to stop and lie down, and for about how long.
  • What you could not do: finish a shift, drive home, make dinner. One line is enough.
  • Whether you missed work or school, or left early.
  • What you took.

Write it the same day if you can. Use your own words. Don't write "prostrating" on every line because you've read it matters; a log that says "left work at 11, in bed until 6, lights off" says more than the word does.

Log the ordinary headaches and the clear days too. A complete record is an accurate one.

Who decides

You describe what happened. Whether an attack counts as prostrating under the schedule is decided by VA, usually with a C&P examiner's report in front of them. VA's manual says your own account can establish prostration if it is credible and medical evidence ties the symptoms to migraine, and that headache journals may be accepted as credible lay testimony. Medical records often don't use the word at all. So a dated record in your own words is worth keeping. A log does not replace a diagnosis.

Two other phrases in the 50% line

"Very frequent"

The schedule gives numbers for 30% (about once a month) and 10% (about one in two months), and none for "very frequent". The M21-1 describes it this way: "Characteristic prostrating attacks, on average, are less than one month apart over the last several months." That guidance does not bind the Board.

"Productive of severe economic inadaptability"

This is about work. Board decisions, citing Pierce v. Principi, 18 Vet. App. 440 (2004), read "productive of" as either producing or capable of producing that result, and note that it does not mean being unable to work at all. Evidence people commonly point to includes sick leave used, unpaid absences and shifts cut short, which is why the log has a "missed work or school" box. The manual also notes that entries for days you weren't due to work generally don't bear on this part.

Keeping the record

The schedule looks at averages "over last several months", so the record needs to run that long. A paper sheet works. So does a phone, if you'd keep that up more easily.
