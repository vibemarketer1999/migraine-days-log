Your migraine C&P exam: what gets asked

VA says the provider at your exam may ask questions from the Disability Benefits Questionnaire, or DBQ, for each condition you claim. The headaches DBQ is public. Read it ahead of time and you'll know the questions, including the four answer choices for how often your attacks happen.

The headaches questionnaire

The form is the Headaches (including Migraine Headaches) Disability Benefits Questionnaire. The public version on VA's site was last updated on 9 July 2024; examiners may work from VA's own version of it. It runs from the evidence reviewed through diagnosis, history and treatment, symptoms, prostrating attacks, other findings, tests and the effect on work, then remarks and the examiner's signature.

It prints VA's definition of the key word: prostrating means "causing extreme exhaustion, powerlessness, debilitation or incapacitation with substantial inability to engage in ordinary activities." (More on the word "prostrating".)

The section on prostrating attacks asks two yes-or-no questions, then how often, "on average … over the last several months":

QuestionAnswer choices if yes
Does the veteran have characteristic prostrating attacks?With less frequent attacks
Once in 2 months
Once every month
Greater than once per month
Does the veteran have completely prostrating and prolonged attacks?

The choices use the same frequency words as the rating schedule (each level explained). The examiner records the answer; a VA rater decides the percentage. The form also asks how long typical head pain lasts (less than 1 day, 1 to 2 days, more than 2 days, or other), which symptoms come with it, such as nausea, light or sound sensitivity and changes in vision, whether your treatment includes continuous medication, and whether the condition affects your ability to work. If it does, the examiner is asked to describe how, with one or more examples.

What VA says about the day itself

  • VA's exam page says "You don't need to bring anything to your exam." New non-VA medical records should be submitted before the appointment (online, through your VSO, or by mail). The provider may look at records you bring but can't submit them for you.
  • Arrive 15 minutes early, in comfortable clothes. If you're late, the provider may cancel the exam.
  • Want a caregiver or family member in the room? Ask the provider. They may be asked to wait outside.
  • It may take 15 minutes, or an hour or more.
  • To reschedule, tell the VA medical center or contractor at least 48 hours ahead (with a contractor, only once per exam).

Missing it matters. Under 38 CFR § 3.655, if you miss an exam without good cause, an original compensation claim is rated on the evidence already on file, which may be less than an exam would have shown. A claim for increase, and some other claims, "shall be denied". If you already receive benefits and miss a reexamination, VA can send a notice and then reduce or stop payment. Good cause includes things like your own illness or hospitalization, or a death in your immediate family. If something comes up, contact VA or the contractor right away.

Why people keep notes anyway

The frequency question asks about an average over several months. Answering that from memory, in a short appointment, is hard. Bad weeks blur together, and good weeks are easy to forget.

Going in, it helps to have these written down:

  • how many migraine days you had in each of the last several months
  • on how many of them you had to stop and lie down
  • how long a typical attack lasts
  • what you take for them
  • dates you missed work, left early or couldn't do your job, with a line about what happened

VA's manual for its raters (M21-1, V.iii.12.A.3) says headache journals may be accepted as credible lay testimony. A log doesn't decide anything on its own; the rater weighs it with everything else. Whether and how to submit yours is a good question for your VSO representative. Whatever you keep, keep it accurate: the bad days as they were, and the ordinary days too.

After the exam

You won't get results at the exam. The examiner writes a report and sends it to VA, and VA sends you a decision letter. To read the report itself, VA says to request a copy with a Freedom of Information Act or Privacy Act request, VA Form 20-10206.
